Sober Companionship

Medically reviewed by Bradley Tourtlotte, MD · Written by the California Treatment Centers clinical team · Last reviewed June 2026

A sober companion is a trained recovery professional who spends time with someone in early recovery in their real life — at home, at work, while travelling — providing structure and accountability during the period when relapse risk is highest and formal treatment has ended.

The transition out of residential treatment is where most recoveries are lost. Inside a program, the day is structured, substances are unavailable, and support is constant. Then it stops, usually abruptly, and the person returns to the exact environment, relationships and routines that surrounded their use. Sober companionship exists to bridge that gap.

What a Sober Companion Actually Does

The work is mostly ordinary, which is the point. A companion builds structure into days that would otherwise be unstructured:

  • Establishing a workable daily routine — sleep, meals, exercise, obligations
  • Getting to meetings, therapy and outpatient appointments, and staying for them
  • Being present at genuinely high-risk moments: the first weekend home, a work event with an open bar, a family holiday, a funeral
  • Removing substances and paraphernalia from a home before someone returns to it
  • Recognising early warning signs — isolation, sleep disruption, secrecy, contact with old suppliers — and naming them before they become a relapse
  • Handling the practical wreckage: appointments missed, bills unpaid, a licence to sort out, a job conversation to prepare for
  • Being awake and available at 2am, when most cravings and most crises actually happen

The Three Common Formats

Live-in

The companion stays in the home, typically for two weeks to several months. This is the most intensive and most expensive arrangement, and it is normally used immediately after residential treatment or detox, or for someone at high risk who cannot safely be alone.

On-Call and Hourly

The companion is present for defined blocks — mornings, evenings, weekends, or specific high-risk events — rather than continuously. This is a common step-down from live-in support as stability builds, and it is considerably more affordable.

Travel and Sober Transport

The companion accompanies someone through travel: a flight to treatment, a work trip, a wedding, a court appearance. Sober transport specifically — safely moving someone from one place to a treatment facility — is a distinct and frequently urgent need, often arranged within hours. Airports, hotels and unstructured time in unfamiliar cities are reliably high-risk, and this is one of the clearest cases where companionship earns its cost.

What a Sober Companion Is Not

Clarity here prevents a lot of disappointment:

  • Not a sponsor. A sponsor is an unpaid peer within a twelve-step fellowship with a specific role in working the steps. A companion is a paid professional. The two are complementary, not interchangeable, and a good companion will actively encourage building genuine fellowship relationships that outlast the engagement.
  • Not a therapist. Companions do not diagnose or provide psychotherapy. If there is a co-occurring mental health condition, that needs proper dual diagnosis treatment alongside — not instead of — companionship.
  • Not a medical provider. Nobody should use companionship as a substitute for medically supervised detox. Alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal can be fatal and requires clinical management.
  • Not security, and not a guarantee. A companion cannot physically prevent an adult from using. What they provide is presence, structure, friction and honest reporting — which is a great deal, but it is not control.

Who It Genuinely Helps

Companionship is not right for everyone, and it is oversold. It tends to be most valuable for:

  • People leaving residential treatment who are returning to an unstructured environment, especially in the first 30 to 90 days
  • People whose work makes conventional aftercare impossible — frequent travel, unpredictable hours, public visibility, or roles where a residential absence cannot be explained
  • People who cannot use sober living because of childcare, professional obligations or family circumstances
  • People with a history of repeated post-treatment relapse, where the pattern is specifically about the transition rather than the treatment
  • Families in crisis who are exhausted, out of their depth, and doing supervision themselves at real cost to their own health

If someone is stable, has strong local support and a workable routine, companionship is probably not the best use of the money.

There Is No Licence for This — Here Is What to Ask

This is the part most companies selling sober companionship will not tell you plainly, so we will. “Sober companion” is not a licensed profession in California. There is no state board, no required examination, and no registry you can search. Anyone may use the title. That does not make the work illegitimate — good companions do genuinely valuable work — but it does mean the burden of vetting falls entirely on you, usually at the worst possible moment.

Relevant credentials do exist, they are just voluntary. Ask which of these the specific person assigned to you holds:

  • California Peer Support Specialist certification. Established under SB 803 and overseen through the Department of Health Care Services, this is the closest thing to a formal state-recognised credential for peer recovery work in California.
  • Recovery coach training — the CCAR Recovery Coach Academy curriculum is the most widely recognised, and a serious companion will usually have completed something comparable.
  • AOD counsellor certification through CCAPP or CADTP, the two main California certifying bodies for alcohol and drug counsellors. This is a higher bar than peer certification and not every companion needs it — but if someone claims to be a “counsellor,” they should hold it.
  • Current CPR and first aid, and naloxone training. A live-in companion working with someone at opioid risk who does not carry naloxone and know how to use it is not adequately prepared.

Questions Worth Asking Before You Hire Anyone

  • Who exactly is coming? Not the agency's credentials — the individual's. Ask for their name, background and certifications before you agree to anything.
  • Is there a background check, and may I see the policy? This person will have access to your home, your family and often your finances.
  • Who are they employed by, and are they insured? An independent contractor with no liability coverage is a different risk than an employed, insured, supervised companion.
  • What is the confidentiality obligation in writing? An independent companion may not be a HIPAA covered entity, so the protection you actually have is whatever the contract says. Get it in writing.
  • Who do they report to, and what happens at 3am? There should be clinical supervision and a defined escalation path, not just a phone number.
  • What happens if there is a relapse? The answer should be a plan, not an ejection.

If a provider is evasive on any of these, that is your answer. Walk away — including from us.

How Pricing Actually Works

Sober companionship is generally not covered by insurance. It is usually classified as a non-clinical support service rather than treatment, so it is typically a private-pay arrangement — a genuine consideration, because it is one of the more expensive forms of recovery support.

Rates vary enormously by experience, format and market, and any figure quoted without knowing your situation is guesswork. What matters more is understanding the structure so you can compare offers honestly:

  • Live-in is normally billed as a flat daily rate covering a defined number of on-duty hours. Ask what happens outside those hours and whether a second companion covers overnight.
  • On-call and hourly work usually carries a minimum engagement — often several hours — so a “one hour” visit rarely costs one hour.
  • Travel is generally the daily rate plus all of the companion's expenses: flights, accommodation, meals, and often travel time.
  • Extras that surprise people: transport mileage, agency placement fees, minimum contract lengths, and cancellation terms. Ask for the full fee schedule in writing before committing.

If cost is the barrier, say so early. Structured sober living combined with intensive outpatient treatment delivers much of the same accountability at a fraction of the price, is frequently insurance-eligible, and is the right answer for far more people than companionship is. We would rather tell you that than sell you something you do not need.

Where It Fits in the Continuum

Companionship works best as one component of a plan, not as the plan. The common effective sequence is medical detox where withdrawal requires it, then residential or PHP, stepping down to IOP — with a companion covering the transition home and tapering off as stability builds. For opioid use disorder, medication-assisted treatment should be part of that plan; companionship supports adherence, it does not replace the medication.

Frequently Asked Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a sober companion and a sober coach? ▾
The terms are used interchangeably and neither is licensed, so the label tells you very little. In practice 'companion' usually implies physical presence — living with or accompanying someone — while 'coach' more often means scheduled sessions, sometimes remote. Ask what the person will actually do and how many hours they will be present, rather than relying on the title.
Does insurance cover a sober companion? ▾
Usually not. Sober companionship is generally treated as a non-clinical support service rather than medical treatment, so it is typically private pay. Structured sober living combined with intensive outpatient treatment provides much of the same accountability, is frequently covered by insurance, and is the more sensible option for many people.
How long do people usually need one? ▾
Most engagements run from two weeks to about three months, with intensity tapering as stability builds. The first 30 days after leaving residential treatment carry the highest relapse risk, so that period is where companionship does most of its work. Open-ended arrangements with no plan to step down are worth questioning.
Is a sober companion licensed or regulated in California? ▾
No. There is no state licence or registry for sober companions, and anyone can use the title. Relevant voluntary credentials do exist — California Peer Support Specialist certification through DHCS, CCAR recovery coach training, and CCAPP or CADTP counsellor certification. Always ask what the specific individual assigned to you holds, along with background check and insurance details.
Can a sober companion stop someone from relapsing? ▾
No, and be sceptical of anyone who suggests otherwise. A companion provides structure, presence, friction and honest reporting, all of which meaningfully reduce risk. They cannot physically prevent an adult from using. What a good companion does is make relapse harder, catch warning signs earlier, and ensure a lapse becomes a recoverable event rather than a catastrophe.
Can you arrange sober transport at short notice? ▾
Often yes — sober transport is frequently needed urgently, such as accompanying someone to a treatment facility. Call 213-321-6518 and we will tell you honestly what is achievable in your timeframe.
